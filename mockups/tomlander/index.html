Mission one, and why the ground changed

Hop from pad A to pad B. The ship starts sitting on A rather than falling out of the sky, so the first thing a player does is take off, which is also the first thing they need to learn. Land well anywhere and the ship just parks and can lift off again; the run only ends on the deck that is pulsing orange.

When that deck is out of frame an arrow appears at the edge of the screen pointing at it, and disappears the moment you can see it again: a marker stuck on top of something already in shot is the decorative status line rule 9 exists to keep off a 120 pixel screen. Its direction comes from the world bearing rotated into the camera's own basis, not from the projection, because a pad behind the camera has no valid projection at all and behind the camera is exactly when an arrow earns its pixels.

The ground was a checkerboard of small quads, and it was the wrong shape for this machine: 288 triangles against the ship's 204, so over half the frame queue went on a flat pattern whose only job was to show that the ship was moving. Shading does that for free. It is 8x8 larger cells now, 128 triangles, each one flat lit off its own normal, with the two diagonals alternating so the tessellation does not line up into rows. Colour comes from height and slope, so a cliff is a cliff. Both pads sit on a flat apron well outside the deck, because the ground you drift over on the way down matters more than the ground you land on.

The pads are blocks now, and that was a bug fix, not a decoration. Flat on the ground they z fought the terrain, which is the one byte depth buffer doing exactly what the engine's own comment warns about: over a 6 to 170 range one depth step is 0.65 units of view distance, and a deck 0.12 above the apron falls in the same step as the apron. They tie, and ties go to whoever drew first, so the pad flickered. A block clears it by two whole steps, and it gives the pad a silhouette to judge a descent against, which a decal never had. Ten triangles each, plated deck, sides carrying the state: orange on the one you are aiming at.

Yes, 16x16 terrain textures are affordable

Toggle it above and watch the millisecond counter. The reason it is cheap is that raster.cpp:234 already interpolates perspective correctly, and spends it on three colour channels. Texturing swaps those three interpolants for two, plus one texel fetch.

256 texels at 4 bits is 128 bytes of flash, and the RP2040's XIP cache is 16 KB, so every fetch after the first is a cache hit rather than a flash read. Lighting is the part that would normally cost: a texel times a per face brightness is three multiplies per pixel on a chip with no FPU. So it is not done per pixel. Each colour and brightness pair is baked into its own shaded copy and a triangle picks one before the loop, which is 32 copies, 16 KB, and zero multiplies per pixel. That is the same trick that makes it viable at 128x128 and pointless at 512.

The flight model is not invented

Gravity, thrust, torque, damping and the hard landing threshold are tom-lander's own constants, lifted out of their per tick form and scaled by 3.2 because this ship is that much bigger in world units. The ratios are untouched, so the number that decides how the game feels carries over exactly: one pod gives 0.70 thrust to weight and all four give 2.81. A single pod cannot hold the ship up. It never could in tom-lander either.

That is what makes scheme A demanding and scheme C forgiving. In A you are always short of lift unless two pods are lit. In C every press lights a pair. Scheme B idles exactly on hover and sinks the moment you tip a nozzle, because vectoring spends lift on going sideways.

The button diamond is the ship seen from above. X is the top button and works the front pod, B the back, Y the left, A the right. A pod lifts its own corner, so you always travel away from the one that is lit. One rule, four buttons, nothing to remember per axis. That does diverge from tom-lander, where keyboard A works the RIGHT thruster so that pressing left takes you left, and both readings are defensible: the original ships an invert setting for this exact pair.

One constant did not survive the port. The original's torque settles at 118 degrees of roll per second off a single pod. That is fine with WASD, an analog pad and a free camera. With four digital buttons at 120 pixels it is a coin flip, so the bench runs at half, about 58 deg/s. Everything else is untouched. If you want it faithful instead, say so and it goes back, but fly it at 6.5 first.

The ship, taken apart

Your picoCAD model is not 110 arbitrary faces. It is five parts, instanced seventeen times, perfectly symmetric about both axes. That is why it demakes cleanly.

Exploded view, drag to orbit. Five distinct solids, seventeen instances, symmetric about both axes. Every one of the seventeen has positive signed volume, so the winding is outward everywhere and normals bake from it with no fixup.

The bug this would have shipped with

picoCAD winds its faces the opposite way to every model already in this repo. All 24 of them have negative signed volume, which is why obj2cpp.face_normal takes the cross product as v x u rather than u x v. All 17 solids in tom.obj are positive.

Fed straight to obj2cpp.py, the result is 0 of 204 normals pointing outward. Lambert collapses to pure ambient and the ship renders as a flat silhouette. It compiles, it runs, it does not warn, and it looks like the model is broken rather than the import. The packer measures the winding per solid and flips it, and says so when it does. After the flip: 204 of 204, and the colours you see on the bench.

One engine change this needs

Renderer3D::draw_mesh takes yaw and pitch. A lander that tilts on two axes at once needs roll as well, so the call wants a third angle. It is a generic addition to a generic mesh drawer, not a game specific special case, so it sits inside rule 7 rather than against it. Cost is one more rotation in the per frame matrix build, which happens once, not per vertex.

Read this before deciding

The engine has no texture mapping. pse::MeshFace is three indices, one flat RGB and a packed normal. Nothing in the rasterizer interpolates a UV, so a texture cannot currently be sampled at all.

That turns out not to matter for this model. Thirteen of the seventeen rectangles are exactly 8×8, which is one picoCAD colour cell, and every rectangle belongs to exactly one part. The sheet is a colour palette drawn as a picture. Sampling each rect and writing the result into the face colour reproduces the model with no visible loss, no per pixel cost, and no flash at all.

The repacked 48×40 sheet is still worth having, and the tool that makes it is written either way. It is what the web build could use if we ever add affine mapping, and it is the thing that proves the bake is faithful. But shipping it to the device buys a blur the panel cannot show: the whole ship is about thirty pixels tall in flight.

tools/pack_picocad_texture.py does the work, and it is written and tested. Give it the .obj and the .png and it fixes the winding, finds the used rects, shelf packs them, rewrites the UVs, writes the small atlas, and emits a .mtl with one material per distinct colour so obj2cpp.py picks the colours up through the path it already has. No engine change, no committed generated file. 

$ tools/pack_picocad_texture.py tom.obj --texture tom.png \
      --atlas tom_atlas.png --out-obj tom.obj --out-mtl tom.mtl

Budget, stated up front

  • Flash roughly 3.3 KB for the ship mesh, under 1 KB for the pad, plus code. Nothing measurable against 12 MB.
  • RAM nothing new. The rasterizer and the frame queue come from pse::shared_rasterizer(), so the console pays for them once.
  • Triangles 204 ship, 128 terrain, 20 pads. Around 350 of the queue's 640, with the terrain culled to a radius rather than drawn whole.
  • Textures 128 bytes each for the 16x16 regolith and deck plate at 4bpp, plus the pre shaded copies that make lighting free per pixel, 512 bytes a copy at RGB565.
  • Cores run_split as usual, one scene, both bands.
