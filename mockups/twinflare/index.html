The one change from the brief, and it needs your call. The brief puts A on the pause menu and also on the double tap boost. Those cannot both be true: a double tap ends with a finger on the button, and a pause menu that opens on the second tap of every boost is unusable. Two answers are built into the bench and you can switch between them while flying:
  • Double tap B (recommended, and what the bench starts on). Boost lives on the throttle. Slamming the throttle twice is the reading that needs no thought at all mid corner, and A is left completely alone as pause. It is also what the original game does, which I did not know when I picked it: in Episode I Racer the Thrust Meter is a readout of your current speed rather than a bar you charge, and at the top of it you arm the boost by briefly releasing the accelerator and pressing it again. The resource you spend is heat. So the throttle double tap is not a compromise forced by the pause button, it is the mechanic.
  • Double tap A, with pause moved to holding A for a third of a second. This is the brief as literally written and it does work. The cost is that every single boost ends with your thumb resting on the pause button, and a hold that fires by accident during a fight for position is a lost race.
Fly both and tell me which. Everything else in the design is indifferent to the answer.

The flight model

Seven things, and each one is in the bench above rather than in this paragraph. Every constant named here is in TUNE in the page source and becomes tuning.hpp unchanged.

1. The hover field is a spring, not a floor

The brief says pods cannot collide with the ground because a force always keeps them above it. That force pushes up and never down, proportional to how far the pod has sunk below its rest height, damped so it does not bounce. Two things fall out of that one rule and neither had to be written separately: the pod breathes over a crest and slams into a dip instead of being glued to the road, and above the rest height there is no field at all, which is why the pod can fly. A ramp is not a special case. It is road that stopped being under you.

2. A gap is an absence, not an obstacle

Nodes flagged as a gap carry no surface. The hover field has nothing to push against, so you fall, and the only way across is to arrive with enough speed and enough nose-up to glide it. There is no trigger volume and no jump code.

3. Glide is lift, and lift costs speed

Nose up in the air gives lift proportional to speed times the angle, capped below gravity so it extends a jump and never cancels it. The same angle bleeds forward speed. Hold the nose up and you clear the gap slower; hold it too far up and you do not clear it at all. That is the whole of the glide skill.

4. The heading swings first and the velocity catches up

Steering commands a yaw rate, the pod's heading eases toward it, and then the part of the velocity that is sideways to the heading bleeds off at a grip rate. The gap between where the pod points and where it is going is the drift, and it is one number: GRIP. The ice track is that number halved and nothing else.

5. Two engines, and the asymmetry is not a special case

Thrust is applied per engine at its own offset from the pod's axis, scaled by that engine's health. With both healthy the torques cancel. As one takes damage it weakens, and the pod starts to pull toward it; when it dies the pull becomes permanent, and the only way to hold a line is to spend most of your steering authority on the correction. Nothing enforces the brief's "flying with one engine will inevitably cause the ship to crash": it falls out of that torque. On a straight you survive. The next corner is the question.

The pull starting before the engine dies is the part worth having. It turns damage from a bar you have to remember to look at into something arriving through your thumb, and a pod that has started drifting left is a pod telling you to hold X. It is also what the original does.

6. The cockpit is a second mass, not a lean

This is the signature of the whole vehicle and the thing a rigid body with a tilt cannot fake. The engines answer the stick at once. The cockpit lags behind them on its cables as a real degree of freedom with its own rate, and then its momentum feeds back into the engines, so a pod flung wide keeps going wide for a beat after the stick has centred and can drag its own engines off line. Three constants and one feedback term, and it is most of what you feel in the bench above through a fast corner. Watch the cockpit: it is visibly off to one side of the line between the engines.

7. The camera is a character

It eases toward the pod's heading rather than being welded to it, so through a corner the pod visibly slides across the frame. It carries a third of the pod's bank, so the horizon tilts. It pulls back when the boost lights. Those three lines are most of what separates this from a flight sim with two engines drawn on it.

Damage, boost, repair

MechanicHow it works
Engine healthTracked separately per engine, as asked. The two bars bottom left are the only place it is shown, because a combined bar would hide the exact state the whole model is about.
BoostDouble tap, and only above 62% of the pod's own top speed, which is what the green light beside the heat bar is for. 1.45 seconds, 1.62x thrust, and the drag ceiling moves with it so the top speed really rises rather than just the acceleration.
HeatRises while boosting, sheds otherwise at a rate set by the pod's cooling stat and the track's air. Past the white line on the gauge both engines cook, evenly, at a rate proportional to how far over it you are.
RedlineAt full the boost locks out and the gauge flashes VENTING until it drops back under 0.42. This is the only hard stop in the game.
RepairFixed rate to both engines, as asked, and it cuts thrust to 34% while it runs. It also pulls the pod toward whichever engine is worse, because repair in the original is a handling penalty rather than a pause button. It cannot resurrect an engine that has already gone: a repair beam is not a spare part.
ExplosionAt zero the engine is gone for the rest of the lap. The binder arc between the engines goes out, which is the clearest possible signal that the pod is now in trouble.
Both goneA wreck. The pod tumbles, then rejoins at the last node with both engines back at half. A three lap race that ends on lap one is a race nobody runs twice.
Other damageGrinding a wall, and landing harder than 26 u/s. A damaged engine smokes, so the state is readable without looking at the bars.

The six pods

Twenty stat points each across six stats, so no pod is an upgrade on another and the choice is a shape rather than a ladder. All six run the same two engine meshes, slim or heavy, recoloured: six distinct pods for two meshes in flash, which is the only version of this that fits.

The four tracks

A track is a ring of nodes and four numbers. The numbers are why these are four games rather than four palettes: gravity, grip, cooling and drag are multipliers the sim applies everywhere, so the moon is not a grey desert, it is a place where a jump goes three times as far, the air brake barely bites and the heat has nowhere to go.

Every track carries the same four features, placed differently: a ramp (road that rises, then stops), a gap (road that is not there), a boost pad, a shortcut (a wider, faster line that costs you the racing line to reach), and walled sections where leaving the road is a scrape rather than a fall. All of them are passable, and the cards prove it with numbers rather than claiming it: worst case jump is the slowest pod on the roster going off the end of the road with no nose-up at all, no glide and no skill, and it clears the widest gap on every track by three times or better. Best case glide is the fastest pod holding the nose up the whole way. The margin between the gap and the worst case is deliberately generous, because a gap that needs a perfect entry is a gap that ends runs rather than one that rewards them; if they play too easy, widening them is one number.

What the engine forced

Six things in this design are the shape they are because of what the engine actually does, not because anyone preferred them. They were found by reading engine/ and the two nearest games before any of this was written, and the bench above is built on all six, so it fails in the same places the device would.

The engine does thisSo the design does this
There is no clipping anywhere. A triangle with one corner past the near plane is dropped whole, not cut.The road is short strips, never long quads, and the nearest strip is anchored to the camera rather than to the road. Dust Rider learned this one already: its comment says the desert vanished.
The near plane is not the near plane. project() rejects at NDC z 0, which is the harmonic mean of the two planes. set_depth_range(0.25, 400) really clips at 0.50.The range is 2 to 170, so the real clip is at 3.95 units, which is where the road behind the pod is allowed to start.
The depth buffer is one byte, hyperbolic. One step is about 0.66 units of view distance in that bracket.The road edge stripes are coplanar strips cut across the road, not stripes floated on top of it. A stripe 0.02 above the tarmac is the same depth value as the tarmac, ties go to whoever drew first, and it simply never appears.
Projection precision decays with absolute world coordinates. Measured: 2 px of error at 500 units, unusable by 5000.A lap here is about 2,200 units around, so every draw is handed coordinates relative to the camera. Absolute coordinates would be fine at the start line and visibly wrong down the back straight, which is the worst kind of bug to chase.
ScreenTriangle stores screen x and y as int16 with no clamp. A wide ground quad near the near plane can project past 32767 and wrap to the other side of the screen.Projected coordinates are clamped before they are stored.
run_split splits at exactly half the screen height, and a road game puts nearly all its fill in the bottom half.Budget on the split buying much less than 2x. Core 1 does most of the work and core 0 waits. Keeping the horizon near the middle of the frame is the only lever, and it is one the camera pitch already pulls.

What it costs

Numbers, not adjectives. The RAM figures are the game's own state on top of what the engine already spends (a Rasterizer is 14,456 bytes and the shared FrameQueue is 21,766, and both are shared, not per game).

Peak triangles a frame-measured in this bench across all four tracks with six pods on screen. The queue holds 640, but that is a RAM ceiling and a drop threshold, not a time budget, and nobody has ever measured the time budget on real hardware
The honest caveat. Triangle counts are the only performance number anyone in this repo can produce without a device, and config.hpp says so itself. This design is within the queue with room to spare, which proves it will not drop geometry. It does not prove it will hit 50 frames a second on a 133 MHz M0+ with no FPU, and the first build's real job is to find that out.
What the bench already caught. The first version of this drew 731 triangles into a queue that holds 640, with the whole pack inside forty units of each other, which is exactly the moment a race is won. The queue does not grow on overflow, it drops triangles and counts them, so a close finish would have shed pieces of pods and the cause would have been almost impossible to attribute. Three fixes, all measured rather than guessed: the engine mesh went from four rings to three (48 triangles to 36, same silhouette, the waist now comes from shading), far road segments dropped their shoulders and their surrounding ground where the horizon wall already covers them, and rivals got three levels of detail with a rank cap on top of the distance test, so only the nearest one is ever drawn in full however close the others get. That is the number in the table above.

Staged so every stage is playable:

  • One. One track, one pod, the flight model, the road, the chase camera. No damage, no rivals, no HUD. This is the stage that answers whether it feels like a podracer, and it is the only stage worth doing twice.
  • Two. Engines, heat, boost, repair, the HUD. The bench above is already this stage.
  • Three. The other three tracks and their four numbers each.
  • Four. Six pods, rivals, position, laps, the results screen, the save.
  • Five. Sound, the title and select screens, the thumbnail, the gallery entry.

What I need you to decide

1. The A button. Double tap B with A as pause, or double tap A with pause on a hold? Fly both in the bench. This is the only one I cannot decide for you.
2. The name. Twin Flare is a placeholder that fits the console menu's 15 character limit and does not borrow anything trademarked. Say the word and it changes; it is one string in three files.
3. Rivals. They run the centreline at a pace of their own and they do not fly the flight model, because a rival that can crash can be lapped by its own physics and none of that is visible from behind you. If you want them racing properly rather than pacing, that is a real cost and a real decision.
4. The wreck. Right now a wreck rejoins at the last node with both engines at half. The alternative is that it ends the race. I picked rejoining because three laps is a long way to lose to one bad landing, but a racer with no failure state is a different game.
5. Is HOARFROST right? It was authored tighter and no autopilot could hold it at half grip, so the corners were opened out and the walled sections widened. It is now the track that slides rather than the track that is impossible. Drive it and say whether it went too far the other way.

Nothing here is committed to the game yet. This page, its four tracks and its six pods are the proposal; say what changes and it changes before a line of C++ exists.
