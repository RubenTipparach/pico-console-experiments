PICOMON

A creature catching adventure for the PicoSystem, drawn the way the DS games were: a real 3D world under a fixed three quarter camera, characters as flat sprites inside it, and menus that are pure 2D on top. Everything on this page renders live at 120 by 120, in four bits per channel, through a software rasterizer that copies what pse::Renderer3D already does. The triangle counter under the screen is the real one, and the camera is not a guess: it was measured out of Black and White, three ways, and it is 31 degrees through a 16.2 degree lens.

Route 1 / the overworld

Walk, talk, and get ambushed. The camera never rotates, which is what keeps the whole thing inside budget.

The world is a tile grid on a flat plane under the camera measured out of Black and White itself: 31 degrees below horizontal through a 16.2 degree lens, yaw locked to the tile grid. That measurement is the section below this one. Locking the yaw is the single biggest saving: with a fixed heading the visible window of tiles is a rectangle in map space, so culling is two comparisons instead of a frustum test, and props can be sorted once at load rather than every frame.

Everything else here is sized from that camera rather than dialled in by eye. Twelve tiles across a 120 pixel screen puts one tile at 10 pixels, which is what makes a 16 pixel tall character sprite draw at exactly 1.0 scale, with no resampling and no soft edges. The camera's height and setback then fall out of the lens: 21.7 tiles up and 36.2 back.

Ground, and why it is cheap

The visible window is 15 tiles across by 31 deep, deeper than it is wide because a 31 degree camera squashes depth by sin(31) = 0.52. Drawing each tile as its own quad is 465 quads, 930 triangles, before a single tree is placed: more than the whole 640 triangle queue, on the ground alone.

Two things fix that. Each row merges runs of the same material into one long quad, and rows get deeper with distance: one tile deep up close, two past six tiles, four past fourteen, because by then a tile is a few pixels tall and the detail was never visible. Together they take the same picture to about 118 quads. The counter next to the screen is measuring exactly this, so you can watch it move as you walk from open grass onto the crossroads.

The cost of merging is that a checkerboard texture would defeat it, every tile would break its own run. So ground detail comes from a handful of scattered decal quads instead: darker grass tufts, stones on the path, ripples on the pond. Detail you place rather than detail you tile.

One more quad sits under all of them. At this pitch the horizon is 257 pixels above a 120 pixel frame, so ground covers every row of the screen and any sky showing through is the map running out, not the camera. Two triangles are cheaper than widening the tile window until the leak stops.

Trees are the other half of the bill, and the long lens is why. A far tree is only about 15 percent smaller than a near one, so it never quietly stops costing anything the way it would under a wide lens. Past four tiles a tree drops its trunk and its second canopy and becomes a single four sided cone: 4 triangles instead of 20.

The whole scene peaks at 448 triangles, which leaves 192 spare in the queue. This is the scene that will fight for the budget, and the two levers are already in the code: the depth of the tile window, and how early a tree drops to its cone.

Characters are sprites, not meshes

The player and every NPC is one camera facing billboard, exactly what project_billboard already returns a position and scale for. Two triangles each if drawn as geometry, or a direct sprite blit with a single depth value, which is what this mockup does and what I would ship. No skinning maths on a chip with no FPU.

The art is real art, not placeholder: 12 by 20 frames authored in art/build_art.py, exported to editable .aseprite files and to PNG strips, and embedded in this page as data URIs so it stays one file. Four sheets, 28 frames, 1.5 KB of PNG. Each character has a four beat walk cycle (step, pass, step, pass) and the profile is drawn facing east and mirrored for west, which is why the hero needs three directions of art rather than four.

Under each character is a darkened ellipse of ground rather than a drawn shadow sprite. It costs no geometry and no art, it reads at 10 pixels per tile, and it is the single thing that stops a billboard looking like a sticker on the screen.

NPCs

  • Villagers stand still and say one thing. Press A while facing them.
  • Trainers own a line of sight straight ahead of them, four tiles long. Cross it and they walk to you and the battle starts, no way out. Their sight line is drawn as a faint tint on the ground while they are unbeaten, so the trap is visible and avoidable rather than a gotcha.
  • The healer in the house restores the party. Free, no currency loop.

Dialogue is the bottom 32 pixels: a filled panel, three lines of 3 by 5 text at four pixel advance, which is 28 characters a line. That is the writing budget, so lines get written to it rather than trimmed to fit later.

Encounters

Tall grass is the only encounter surface. Each step onto a grass tile adds to a counter; when it crosses a threshold the screen flashes and the battle scene takes over. Walk into the patch on the left to trigger one here.

Battle

Two low poly creatures on a lit arena, plates over the top, a four way menu at the bottom. The 3D is here because it animates, not because it is flashy.

Each creature is a UV blob of 80 triangles with box ears, a tail, and eyes drawn as projected dots rather than geometry. That last one matters: two spheres for eyes would cost more triangles than the head. Projecting two points and writing four pixels reads better at this size and costs nothing.

The whole scene peaks at 281 triangles: two creatures, two pads, three merged ground bands, and a nine tree backdrop. That leaves over half the queue spare, which is deliberate. Attack animations spawn extra geometry and the budget has to survive the worst frame, not the average one. The ember arc here is drawn as projected points rather than geometry, which is why firing a move does not move the counter at all.

Why meshes and not sprites

Sprites would be cheaper per frame but not per byte. A creature that has to face two ways, lunge, flinch, and faint needs a sheet per pose per direction; the blob does all of it with one mesh in flash and a rotation. It also means the camera can push in on an attack, which is the single thing that makes a turn feel like it landed.

Types

Six types, not eighteen. A player has to hold the whole chart in their head on a screen with no room to show it: EMBER, TIDE, LEAF, SPARK, STONE, MIND. The first five are a ring where each beats the next, and MIND is even against everything but takes double from itself. One rule, no table to memorise.

Damage, in integers

No floats anywhere in the turn resolution:

dmg = ((2 * lvl / 5 + 2) * power * atk / def / 50 + 2) * mult / 4 * rng / 255

where mult is 8 for super effective, 4 for neutral, 2 for resisted, and rng is 217 to 255. Everything is 32 bit integer maths, evaluated left to right so the intermediate never overflows.

Controls

Menu is a 2 by 2 grid: FIGHT, BAG, MON, RUN. A confirms, B backs out. No button prompts on screen, per the project rule, and none are needed because every button that does anything does the obvious thing.

Catch

The one moment the game is actually about. It gets its own animation budget and no text until it is over.

Press A to throw. The ball travels a parabola in world space, the creature shrinks and washes to white as it is drawn in, the ball drops and settles, then wobbles. Three wobbles and it clicks. One wobble and it breaks out.

The odds, and the tell

Gen 3 style, adapted to a chip with no FPU:

a = (3 * maxhp - 2 * hp) * rate * ball / (3 * maxhp), then multiplied by status (sleep and freeze double it, everything else is 1.5 as a shift and add), clamped to 1 to 255.

The shake check is normally a fourth root, which is a non starter here. Instead a indexes a 32 entry lookup table in flash, 64 bytes, giving the shake threshold directly. Four rolls against it: pass all four and it is caught. The table is generated once by a build script from the real formula, so the maths is right and the device never does it.

The wobble count is the only feedback. No percentage, no odds display, nothing to read. Watching the ball is the game.

Balls

  • Pico Ball, multiplier 1, the one you always have.
  • Great Ball, 1.5, as a shift and add.
  • Dusk Ball, 3 in caves and at night, 1 otherwise. Cheap to implement, changes where people hunt.

Bag

Pure 2D, zero triangles, and the place where the 3 by 5 font earns its keep.

Three pockets on a tab row: BALLS, MEDICINE, KEY. Left and right change pocket, up and down move the cursor, A uses, B leaves. Eight rows fit at once with a description line underneath, and the pocket a stack lands in is decided by the item, so nothing has to be sorted by hand.

The whole bag is 64 slots of two bytes, an item id and a count. 128 bytes of save state, and no dynamic allocation anywhere.

Item names get 12 characters at four pixel advance. That is the constraint the names are written to, which is why they are short rather than truncated with an ellipsis.

Party

Six slots, each one row. The screen is too small for portraits, so the icon does the recognising.

Every row is a 16 by 16 icon, a name, a level, and an HP bar that changes colour at a half and a quarter. The bar is the whole point: it has to be readable at a glance from across a room, so it is a solid block with a hard edge, not a gradient.

A opens the slot for a summary and move list, B leaves, and holding A then moving picks a slot up to reorder the party. Reordering matters because slot one is what gets sent out.

A party member is 32 bytes: species, level, experience, four move ids with PP, current HP, status, and five stat experience counters. Six of them is 192 bytes of save state.

The camera, measured

The first version of this page guessed the angle and it read as top down. So the angle was measured out of the game instead, three ways, and the three agree.

The surprise is that the pitch was never the main problem. The lens was. A wide lens at a shallow pitch still looks near vertical at the bottom of the frame, because the bottom of a 60 degree lens is looking 30 degrees further down than the middle. Black and White uses a very long lens, so every row of the screen sees the ground at nearly the same angle, and that uniformity is most of what makes it read as a world rather than a plan.

1. Where the horizon is

A frame of the Nacrene City museum plaza has a paved road whose two edges run along the world depth axis. Fitting a line to each edge gives 0.62 and 0.72 pixels of RMS error, and the two lines meet at x = 128.26, y = -314.9 on a 256 by 192 frame.

That intersection is the vanishing point of the depth axis, so two things fall straight out of it. Its x lands on 128.26 against a frame centre of 128.0, which says the camera yaw is locked to the tile grid to within a quarter of a pixel. And its y is the horizon, which gives f * tan(pitch) = 96 + 314.9 = 410.9 px.

One frame of a flat plane cannot do better than that. Every focal length and pitch satisfying that product projects the ground identically; they differ only in how tall vertical things look. So the pitch needs one more fact from somewhere else.

2. What the lens is

pret's pokeplatinum decompilation carries Gen IV's whole field camera table in degrees, and DSPRE reads the same table out of the ROM and agrees with it byte for byte. The stored vertical half angle is 8.0914306640625 degrees, so the full vertical field of view is 16.18 degrees. Gen V's camera config is a NARC whose format nobody has published, but Gen V inherited the field engine, and the number checks out below.

Feeding that lens into the measured horizon gives the pitch:

f, on a 192 tall frame675.3 px96 / tan(8.0914 deg)
tan(pitch)0.6088410.9 / 675.3
pitch31.32 degbelow horizontal

3. An independent check, and a coincidence that is not one

Bulbapedia's top down map render of the same Route 3 scene turns out to be a tilted orthographic view, not a straight down one. Two repeating decorations in it, a flower hedge and a forest, both repeat about 0.80 as far along depth as across width, which puts the map's own tilt at 53.5 degrees.

The Day Care building appears in both that map and the game frame. The ratio of its wall height to its wall width is 1.43 times larger in the game frame than in the map, and that ratio is exactly cos(pitch_game) / cos(pitch_map), because the width cancels. That gives 31.75 degrees, within half a degree of the horizon result.

And the number it lands on is worth noticing. Platinum's documented camera sits 571.97 units above its target and 342.98 behind it, which is 59.05 degrees. Black and White's measured 31.3 is the complement, 30.95, to within the measurement error: the same camera vector with its two components swapped. Gen V appears to have kept Gen IV's projection exactly and swung the camera down to the other side of the diagonal.

Black and White shows 15.8 tiles across its 256 pixel screen, its own 16 pixels per tile. This screen is 120 wide, and holding a character sprite at about one tile puts 12 across instead. That is the one number here chosen rather than measured, and it is a consequence of the smaller screen.

How to tell whether it is right

  • A square world tile at the middle of the screen is about twice as wide as it is deep, because sin(31) = 0.52.
  • The horizon is 257 pixels above a 120 pixel frame, so it is never in shot and ground fills every row.
  • A tree at the far edge of the window is only about 15 percent smaller than the same tree next to the player. Under the old 60 degree lens it was less than half the size, and that falling away is exactly what a wide lens does and this camera does not.
  • Two ground lines that run along the depth axis meet on the vertical centre line of the screen, not off to one side.

What this costs the device

A long lens flattens the depth range, which matters for a 16 bit depth buffer: with the camera 42 tiles from the player and the whole scene inside a few tiles of that, the projected depth values sit in a narrow band. That wants checking against the rasterizer's fixed point depth range before anyone trusts the z buffer to sort two creatures standing near each other. It is on the list, not solved.

The battle scene deliberately does not use this camera. A battle is a different shot, so it runs a wider 30 degree lens from lower down, which is how Black and White stages its own battles against its field camera. It is the one place this page leaves the measured numbers, and it says so rather than drifting quietly.

The art pipeline

Character art is authored, versioned and rebuilt by a script, and it exports to the format an artist would actually want to open.

mockups/picomon/art/build_art.py holds every character as character-per-pixel strings, which is what makes a change to a sleeve show up in a diff rather than as a binary blob nobody can review. Running it writes, for each of the four sheets:

  • .aseprite, a real Aseprite file with three layers (fill, shade, outline) and one animation tag per direction. It opens in Aseprite and in LibreSprite, and the layer split is by what a pixel is for, so recolouring a jacket or softening an outline is one layer's work.
  • .png, a horizontal frame strip, which is what the page actually loads.
  • sheets.json and sheets.js, the frame size, frame count and tag ranges, and the same strips as data URIs for embedding.

Aseprite is a paid GUI application and could not be installed here, so the .aseprite files are written directly by art/aseprite.py, a small encoder for the documented v1.3 format: RGBA cels, zlib compressed, named layers, tags. Every file is parsed back and checked against its own PNG before it ships, so a malformed chunk cannot get out.

Two constraints shaped the art rather than decorating it. Every colour is a multiple of 0x11, so it survives four bits per channel unchanged: a colour that does not is a colour that shifts on the device and nowhere else, which is the worst place to find out. And the frame is 20 pixels tall because the camera puts a tile at 10 pixels, so a character is exactly two tiles and draws at 1.0 scale with no resampling. Change the tiles across the screen and the art has to be reauthored, not rescaled.

A sheet only carries the poses its character needs. The villagers face the camera and never turn, so they have one direction. The trainer owns a line of sight and has to be seen from behind, so it has two. Only the hero needs three, and west is the east art mirrored.

The parts I would cut first

Day and night

A tint on the ground colours and a clock in the save block. Cheap, and it makes Dusk Ball mean something. Keep unless it drags.

Trading

No radio on the PicoSystem. A code you type in could move a creature between saves, but that is a second game's worth of UI. Cut.

Evolution

Two meshes per line instead of one, so it roughly doubles the mesh flash. Worth it for maybe six lines, not for all 24 species.

Six party slots

Four would fit the party screen better and halve the save block. Six is only there because it is what people expect.

What I need you to decide

  1. Sprites or meshes for creatures in battle Meshes cost triangles and buy animation and a moving camera. Sprites cost flash and buy art that looks exactly like what you drew. This mockup argues for meshes; it is the biggest single call on the page.
  2. Six types or three Six is a ring you can hold in your head. Three is rock paper scissors and needs no explaining at all. Six shown here.
  3. How big is the world Six zones of 64 by 32 is about two hours of play and fits everything above. More zones is mostly more tile data and more writing, not more engine.
  4. Does the fixed camera bother you Locking the yaw is where most of the budget comes from. A rotatable camera is possible but it costs the cheap culling and the pre sorted props, and I would want something back for it.
  5. Catching only, or catching and gyms Gyms are trainers with a bigger party and a locked door, so they are almost free. The romantic date from ideas.md is not free and is not mocked up here.
